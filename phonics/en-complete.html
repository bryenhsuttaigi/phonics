Prologue

1 · What Hokkien is, and who speaks it

Hokkien is the everyday language of southern Fujian and of Taiwan, carried by generations of migration into Singapore, Malaysia, the Philippines and Indonesia. Tens of millions of people speak it. In Taiwan it is the language of the morning market and the temple courtyard, of the taxi driver taking a call from his mother, of a great deal of the island's music and film — the language people fall into the moment they stop being formal.

One thing to be clear about from the start: Hokkien is not a variety of Mandarin in any sense useful to a learner. The two are related, but a Mandarin speaker does not understand Hokkien any more than a French speaker understands Romanian. Knowing Mandarin will help you with vocabulary and with written characters; it will not tell you how a single Hokkien word sounds.

Three things about the sound system are worth knowing before you turn the page, because they are exactly the three this book spends its time on:

  • Seven tones. Mandarin has four. Hokkien has seven — and, unlike Mandarin, they shift systematically the moment words are strung together.
  • Syllables that close. Hokkien kept the final -p, -t, -k and -m consonants that Mandarin lost a thousand years ago. English still has them, at the end of cap, cat, back, ham.
  • Voiced consonants. Hokkien has a true voiced b and g. Mandarin doesn't. English does — which means you already own a sound most Mandarin speakers have to be taught.

Hold on to that last one. It is the first sign of something this book will keep coming back to: an English-speaking mouth is not a handicap here. In several places it is an advantage.

2 · Why you're here

People arrive at this language by very different roads.

Some have a grandmother whose Mandarin was always a second language, and who is most herself in Hokkien. Some married into a family where dinner-table conversation switches over the moment the meal gets comfortable, and would like to be inside the joke rather than beside it. Some fell for the food, the temples, the night markets, the films, the ballads. Some are heritage speakers — they understood every word as a child, answered in English, and now find they can follow a conversation but can't produce a sentence. Some are linguists, drawn by a tone system that does things Mandarin's cannot.

Whatever the road, most people hit the same wall at the same place. The available materials were written either for trained linguists or for readers who already speak the language, and both assume things you don't have:

  • They teach tones as numbers. "This word is tone 7." A number is a label, and a label cannot sing. You memorize the digits and get back not one scrap of pitch. Someone who already speaks the language can match a new word against a word they know; you have nothing to match against.
  • They spell with letters that don't act like letters. You know how to read English. Then you're told this particular p is not the p you know, and that this h changes the letter in front of it rather than making a sound of its own. Your reading reflex — the single most automatic skill you brought to the table — becomes the thing most likely to make you wrong.
  • They want you equipped before you begin. A special keyboard or input method to type the marks. A set of memorized reference words to identify a tone. Every prerequisite turns somebody away at the door.

Across dozens of everyday words, this alphabet trains your reading instinct into a set of pronunciations that have nothing to do with the language your instinct came from. And the harder you work at it, the more thoroughly you learn the wrong reflex.

This is worth being fair about, because it is not incompetence. That spelling was born roughly two hundred years ago as Pe̍h-ōe-jī, devised by foreign missionaries, and was later standardized by linguists into today's Tâi-lô. In its own era it served missionaries and trained linguists — evangelism, literacy campaigns, academic transcription. Aligning with an English reader's instincts was never on the list, and could not have been: it predates the readers it is now handed to. Used by someone who has been taught what each letter stands for, it works. Handed cold to someone who reads English, it does the opposite of what a spelling is for.

That is the whole reason this system exists. Not to argue with anyone — to give the English-reading eye a spelling it can obey.

(And "hard to learn" is only the first layer. Whether that two-hundred-year-old analysis actually recorded every sound of Hokkien accurately is a separate and much sharper question. The tones chapter gives a concrete answer, on a point as basic as how many tones a closed syllable can carry.)

None of that is a statement about your ability. It's a statement about what those materials were built for — and they were not built for you.

3 · What this book does differently

This spelling was designed backwards from the reader. It starts by asking what an English-speaking brain does automatically, and then arranges the letters so that doing the automatic thing produces the right Hokkien sound. Instinct, in other words, isn't luck — it's something you design for. Three things get borrowed.

First, the tones borrow your musical ear. Stripped of terminology, a Hokkien tone is a pitch. Three heights carry the whole system: Do (low), Mi (mid), So (high) — the 1, 3 and 5 that give the system its name. Every tone is one of those heights or a move between two of them, written with a mark whose shape is the move: _ sits low, ¯ rides high, / climbs, \ drops. You are not memorizing a code. You are reading a staff and singing it.

Second, the letters borrow your phonics reflex. Every letter in this system comes with two notes: an anchor (an English word where your mouth already makes the sound) and a landmine (the spelling that would have set your instinct off in the wrong direction). "ah" as in Bahamas, never a bare a, which your eye turns into the vowel of cat. "aw" as in law, never a bare o, which your eye turns into oh. Nothing here has to be memorized against your instincts, because the spelling was chosen to agree with them.

And in the places where English gives you something for free, this book simply takes it. The b of boy and the g of go — where your vocal folds start buzzing before your lips open — are the Hokkien voiced stops, complete, no adjustment needed. The p, t and k inside spy, star, skate, where English quietly strips the puff of air, are the Hokkien unaspirated stops, also complete. You have been making these sounds your whole life without being told they had names.

Third, typing borrows the keyboard already in front of you. No fonts, no input method, no diacritics. Seven tones, seven characters you can type right now: _ + ¯ / ~ \ . — and that final period does exactly what a staccato dot does in music: short, struck, released.

Add the three together and you get the whole design brief: hand the letters a coat that reflexively shows the right sound, and hand the tones straight to the ear.

4 · How to use this book

The claim this system makes is that it has no prerequisites. You need an ear that can tell high from low — you have one. You need to be able to read the Roman alphabet — you have that. You need a keyboard — it's under your hands. Everything else is in these pages.

The order is deliberate, and it is not the order most books use:

  1. The tone marks first — before a single consonant. Tones are where learners are usually abandoned, so they get taught first, while your attention is fresh.
  2. Initials — the consonants that open a syllable, each anchored to an English word.
  3. Rimes — everything after the initial: the vowels, the nasals, and the four ways a syllable can close.
  4. Tones in full — singing all seven, then what happens to them in connected speech, which is where Hokkien starts sounding like Hokkien.
  5. Putting it together — real sentences, read aloud.

Two habits are worth building from the first page. Read everything out loud — this is a spelling system for speaking, and it teaches your mouth nothing while you read silently. And look at the tone mark before you make a sound: your English instinct will want to apply its own intonation to a word, and the mark is the instruction that overrides it. See the spelling, check the mark, then speak.

Chinese characters appear throughout, next to the examples, so you can carry a word over to a dictionary or show it to someone. You never need to read them to use this book. The spelling is the pronunciation; the characters are a courtesy.

By the last chapter you will be able to look at any word written in this system — one you have never seen before — and say it correctly on the first try. That is the entire promise, and it is a reasonable one, because nearly everything it depends on is something you already knew before you opened the book.

First contact: the tone marks

(This chapter is deliberately short. Its only job is to make the little marks beside the examples readable, so the next two chapters make sense. How to sing each tone accurately, and what happens to them in connected speech, waits for the tones chapter.)

Why this comes first

Turn to the initials or the rimes chapter and you'll see examples carrying small marks: tsai_, chin_, ttawk+, hai\. Those marks are the tones — and in Hokkien one syllable sung at different pitches is a different word with a different meaning.

The good news is worth saying plainly: a Hokkien tone is a musical pitch. There is nothing mystical about it. Think Do, Mi, So. This page only teaches you to recognize the marks — if you can hum, you can already produce what they're asking for.

Three reference pitches: Do, Mi, So

Get three heights, and everything else is built out of them:

  • Do (low) — pressed down low, sitting at the bottom of your throat.
  • Mi (mid) — flat, mid-range. This is where Hokkien lives by default: a sentence with nothing special going on stays here. It also happens to sit near your neutral speaking pitch in English.
  • So (high) — one step above Mi, hoisted up and held level. Don't hunt for it by talking; hum it. Sing do-mi-so and the last note is the one.

Those three heights are the three marks: low _ (Do), mid + (Mi), high ¯ (So). The low mark sits on the floor of the line, the high mark rides along the top, the mid mark holds the middle. Wherever the mark sits is where you sing.

All seven tones in one table

Hokkien has seven tones. You already have three of them (low, mid, high); the other four are movements between those same three heights. In the table below, the left column names the shape (the name tells you what it does), then the mark, then the traditional tone number, then how to sing it:

Drill those seven words until they run by themselves — Low, Mid, High, Rise, Dip, Fall, Clip. Say them in order, out loud, the way a music student runs a scale before playing anything. Once the sequence is reflex, the marks stop being symbols you decode and start being notes you read.

If you've studied Mandarin, the "traditional name" column is a free bridge: Tones 1, 2, 3 and 4 line up with the four Mandarin tones — High, Rise, Dip, Fall — so borrow them outright at no cost. Low and Mid are the two Mandarin doesn't have; learn those as themselves. (Mandarin has a light tone too, though it uses it far less.)

The shape of each mark helps you: _ sits on the floor, ¯ rides the ceiling, + holds the middle; / climbs, \ drops, ~ sinks then hooks, . taps once and stops. You can guess the pitch contour from the mark alone.

Of the seven, only the Dip has no ready-made English equivalent. Rise is your questioning intonation, Fall is your flat "No.", and the level tones are just heights — all borrowable. But that sink-then-hook shape isn't a move English makes, so it's the one to imitate from a recording rather than read off the page.

Tone 1 (High) is marked ¯. When typing you may leave it off — an unmarked syllable is Tone 1 — but in teaching material and anything you publish, mark it. (Exact pitch values, the traditional "seven tones in eight slots" business, and the all-digit typing codes are in the tones chapter.)

That's enough for this page. You can now read the marks beside the examples, which means the initials and rimes chapters are open to you. How to sing the seven accurately, and how they change when words are strung together, is the tones chapter's job — and there we'll do it by singing and by moving your body, one step at a time.

Initials (Consonants)

1 · The twelve basic initials

Every place of articulation in Hokkien hides three brothers: an unaspirated voiceless stop, an aspirated voiceless stop, and a voiced one. Mandarin has no voiced series at all, which is why the bopomofo alphabet has no letter for it — but you produce those sounds every day. We'll walk the three families in order (lips, tongue tip, tongue back), and every one is matched to a movement your mouth already makes in English.

The lip family: pp / p / b

pp ㄅ — unaspirated voiceless

This one sits in a place English never labels: the air is locked off, and the vocal folds aren't running either. Hard to describe — but you say it constantly. It's in apple. Say "apple" with your palm in front of your mouth: the pp in the middle closes and opens with no puff of air at all. That locked, unpuffed, unvoiced sound is the authentic Hokkien ㄅ. So we write it doubled, pp — your eye sees the double letter, your brain flips into "apple mode", and the air stays locked. Examples: 包 "wrap" ppao¯, 半 "half" ppuan_.

An even cleaner anchor is the s-cluster family — spit, spy, spin. That's not a coincidence; it's one of the most stable rules in English phonetics: after s, the p, t or k loses its aspiration. Test it yourself. Palm in front of your mouth, say "pie" — a puff lands on your hand. Now say "spy" — same letter p, and nothing lands at all. The s swallowed the puff, and what's left is exactly what Hokkien wants. Notice what this means: among the twenty-six letters of the alphabet, English has no letter for this sound. It only ever appears as a side effect of p hiding behind an s; English never treated it as a sound in its own right, so it never gave it a letter. That's why we had to intervene and double it — pp, a spelling English doesn't use, to pull a sound out of hiding. (We don't write it b; that letter is reserved for the voiced one below.)

p ㄆ — aspirated voiceless

This is the big puff you release at the front of pie. Palm in front of your mouth, say "pie", feel the air hit. When you see a single p, blow. (Never ph — you'd read it as the /f/ of photo, which isn't Hokkien.) Examples: 打 "hit" pah., 品 "quality" pin\.

b ㄅㄏ — voiced

Voiced stops are the ones Mandarin lacks: the vocal folds have to start vibrating before the burst. Bopomofo has no symbol for it (Mandarin has no such sound, so nobody ever invented one), so this system marks it by hanging a small ㄏ under the letter — ㄅㄏ — purely as a "this one is voiced" flag; the ㄏ is not an h sound. You'll see the same device on ㄉㄏ and ㄍㄏ later.

For you, none of that matters, because the sound is simply the b of boy and book. Take it as it is. Examples: 肉 "meat" bah., 舞 "dance" bu\, 馬 "horse" beh\. (We don't spell it bh — that combination doesn't occur in English and invites misreading.)

One warning that matters more for teachers than for learners: some instructors, to save time, teach this b as if it were the same as ㄅ. It isn't. ㄅ (pp) is unaspirated and unvoiced; b is voiced. Two different boxes, never to be merged.

m ㄇ

The m of man and mother. Read it as written; there's no trap. Examples: 毛 "hair" maw~, 命 "life" ming+.

The tongue-tip family: tt / t

tt ㄉ — unaspirated voiceless

Hiding inside star and stop — strip off the s and keep the air-locked t. Same logic as the pp in apple. Examples: 大 "big" ttua+, 踏 "step on" ttah+, 淡 "bland" ttahm+. That last one closes on -m, and here's a small piece of good news: you'll do it better than most Mandarin speakers will. They tend to drop a final m; you see -m and honestly shut your lips, because English words end that way all the time.

t ㄊ — aspirated voiceless

The puff at the front of top and tea, as forceful as the p. (Never th — you'd put your tongue between your teeth, as in think or the, and that sound is not in Hokkien.) Examples: 拖 "drag" tua¯, 殺 "kill" tai~.

ㄉㄏ would be written d — voiced, but no Hokkien word uses it

The old rhyme dictionaries of Middle Chinese record this consonant; both Hokkien and modern Mandarin have since lost it. In plain terms: the sound exists perfectly well — English d is it — but no Hokkien word needs the letter, so the box stays empty. We keep the chair rather than seating someone in it by force. (There's a nice detail here: this voiced d shares its place of articulation with n and l, and sounds close to them. That is exactly why the traditional alphabet groups ㄉㄊㄋㄌ together — they were always one family.)

n ㄋ and l ㄌ

The n of noon, the l of look. Read them as written. Examples: 若 "if" nah+, 鬧 "noisy" nao+, 路 "road" law+, 來 "come" lai~.

The tongue-back family: kk / k / g

kk ㄍ — unaspirated voiceless

Hiding inside skate and sky; drop the s and keep the air-locked k. Same logic as pp and tt, and by now you should see the pattern: English s-clusters are the secret textbook for the Hokkien unaspirated stops. Examples: 咬 "bite" kkah+, 工 "work" kkahng¯.

k ㄎ — aspirated voiceless

The puff at the front of kite and kiss. (Not kh — not an English spelling.) Examples: 可 "may" kuh\, 開 "open" kai¯.

g ㄍㄏ — voiced

Same principle as b: the g of go and game, folds buzzing before the release. Examples: 我 "I" gua\, 五 "five" gaw+, 餓 "hungry" guh+. (We write a single g, never gh — English gh is either silent, as in light, or an /f/, as in laugh. Both would send you the wrong way; a clean g lands you in the right place.)

There's a standard self-test where you put a hand on your throat: say 我 gua\ and feel it buzz like a phone on vibrate (voiced), then say 咬 kkah+ and feel it sit still before the sound (voiceless). It's a useful drill — for Mandarin speakers. You don't need it. Your b and g are already voiced and have been since you learned to talk. Just don't let the unfamiliar surroundings talk you out of them. If you ever do want to check the mechanism: with the lips sealed or the tongue back planted, let the folds hum for an instant before you release. The buzz comes first, not as a patch afterwards.

h ㄏ

The h of how and hot. 好 "good" huh\, 雨 "rain" haw+. Read it as written.

That's twelve Hokkien initials: pp, p, b, m, tt, t, n, l, kk, k, g, h. For most learners, running down that list a few times out loud is enough. 2 · The alveolar and palatal set: ts · ch · s · z

This region sounds like seven different consonants, but there are only 3 + 1 letters to learn: ts, ch, s, z. The structure is simple — ts, ch and s share one rule (each splits into two sounds automatically depending on the vowel that follows), while z stands alone and never splits. You don't have to memorize which sound goes with which letter. Your mouth does the sorting for you.

ts · ch · s: three letters that split themselves

ts ㄗ / ㄐ

ts is the zz in the middle of pizza — tongue tip planted, flicked open, gone. No puff of air. You produce this sound constantly; you have just never had it at the front of a word. Move it to the front and it's the Hokkien ㄗ.

Now watch it split. With -ai or -eh it stays where it is: 再 "again" tsai_, 多 "many" tseh+. But put a high front i after it and the body of your tongue gets pushed up toward the roof of your mouth automatically, and ts turns into the "j"-ish sound of ㄐ: 真 "true" tsin¯. You issue no instruction. Your mouth palatalizes on its own.

Which gives you the most labour-saving fact in this chapter: ㄗ and ㄐ are the same consonant. ㄐ isn't a second sound to memorize; it's what ts becomes when a high front vowel follows.

(Don't anchor on cats — a word-final ts is sloppy in English and often leaks air. Anchor on pizza. Don't write dz either: d is the uniform of the voiced series, and this consonant is voiceless. And once it palatalizes, don't reach for j — you'd burst into the voiced sound of jeep or jet, which turns a clean voiceless ㄐ into something else entirely.)

ch ㄘ / ㄑ

ch is the aspirated version of ts — same flick of the tongue tip, but with a puff of air riding out on top of it, like the start of check. The splitting rule is identical. With -ai or -eh: 菜 "vegetable" chai_, 切 "cut" cheht.. With a high front i it palatalizes by itself into ㄑ — the start of cheese: 秤 "scale" chin_.

Why not c? Because c is the least honest letter you own: sometimes a k (cat, cot, cut), sometimes an s (cent, city), and you never know which until you've read the whole word. Neither aspirated sound goes anywhere near it; both use the dependable ch. Why not tsh? Other systems spell the aspirated ts that way, and it invites you to read two stacked letters as some compound; here one ch does the job, and that's one fewer thing to learn. Why not q? In English q never stands alone — it drags a u along behind it (queen, quiz) — so borrowing it would leave you wondering every time whether a u belongs there.

s ㄙ / ㄒ

s is a fricative: nothing is stopped or flicked, the air just runs out through the groove. It's the s of song and sun. Same rule again — with -ai or -eh it stays put: 獅 "lion" sai¯, 洗 "wash" seh\. With a high front i it rides up into ㄒ, the flat-tongued sound you make in see and sit: 新 "new" sin¯.

(Not sh. The sh of shoe is rounded at the lips, /ʃ/, and that rounding destroys the flat, blade-against-the-palate quality Hokkien wants. One s covers the whole territory; you never need x or sh, because the vowel does the lifting for you.)

z ㄖ — on its own, no splitting

First, a caution about the reference symbol. ㄖ is borrowed from the bopomofo chart as a label, but this is not the retroflex ㄖ of Mandarin. Hokkien has no retroflex sounds at all. Do not curl your tongue back. What you want is the plain, uncurled, voiced friction of English z — zero, zone. Using r would curl your tongue (rain); using j would burst it into a stop. Neither is right. And unlike ts, ch and s, z does not split with the vowel: it has one job and holds it.

Note also that z is reserved for ㄖ and is not used for ㄗ, even though the pizza sound has a certain z-ish quality to an English ear. If 走 "walk" were spelled zau, you'd read the z as in zero and produce a different word. One letter, one sound: ㄗ goes to ts, and z stays with ㄖ. Examples: 日 "day" zit+, 熱 "hot" zeht+.

3 · ng: the sound you think you can't make

Here is a sound English keeps at exactly the opposite end of a word from where Hokkien wants it. Your ng only ever ends things — sing, song, long — and never opens them. Hokkien likes it at the front. That sounds impossible until someone shows you the trick, which takes about ten seconds.

(One principle first, so you don't get pulled off target: always anchor this on the back-of-the-mouth nasal in song and long. Not sing, king or ring — in current American English those endings have drifted forward and sound closer to an "-in", which will bend the round, back-of-the-throat Hokkien nasal out of shape.)

At the front of a syllable, ng does two different jobs. (It has four jobs in total, counting its life as a rime; those other two are in the rimes chapter.)

Job one: ng opens the syllable

The straightforward case — ng sits right at the front: 碗 "bowl" ngua\, 餡 "filling" ngah+, 圓 "round" ngee~, 影 "shadow" ngia\.

How do you produce it? Do this once and you'll have it. Say the English words "song-ah" at a normal speed. Get comfortable. Now bite off the opening s and keep only "ng-ah". That's it — the nasal you had at the end of a word is now at the beginning of one.

(Why drag the ah along? Because an ng on its own is a sound that closes and dies in the throat, with nowhere to go next — and in Hokkien a word-initial ng must always glide onward into a vowel. The ah is the receiving vowel: it forces you to push the nasal forward and hand it off instead of letting it sit and hum. We start with ah because it opens the mouth widest and is easiest to feel. Swap in another vowel and you have ngua "bowl", ngee "round".)

Do it a few more times, out loud, along with a recording, and it will simply live in your mouth.

Job two: the nasalizing initial — ng wedged between consonant and vowel

In the second job, a consonant comes out first, then the ng, then the glide into a vowel: 聲 "sound" sngia¯, 聽 "listen" tngia¯, 雅 "elegant" gngah\. Telling the two jobs apart is easy: if there's still a vowel to glide into after the ng (the -ia of sngia), it's this kind — the consonant and the ng together spell a single sound.

There's a useful piece of body-mechanics for producing it. When you see an opening like sng- or tng-, imagine that at the instant the syllable starts you shove a burst of air up into your nose, and then let the consonant and vowel come out through it. (That's a physical cue for finding the feeling, not an analysis of articulation. Do it that way and you'll be right the first time you open your mouth.)

The design principle behind the spelling is one sentence: what you see is what you say. The main consonant (g, s, t…) and the nasal (ng) are both written out; nothing is abbreviated away. You see gng, and both the voiced g and the nasal are there waiting for you. Produce the consonant, send the air through your nose at the same time, and you're done.

And this isn't a list of special words. Every initial in Hokkien can be nasalized. You never have to memorize which words nasalize and which don't; when nasalization is called for, you throw the switch we just described — put the nasal first, and let the air go through the nose from the first instant. Nasalization isn't a long column of exceptions. It's a switch you can flip any time.

A note you'll meet again in the rimes chapter: words like 卵 "egg" nng+, 光 "light" kkng¯ and 酸 "sour" sng¯ also have a consonant followed by ng, but with no vowel after it. In those, the ng is functioning as the rime — it is the whole rest of the syllable — and it is not a nasalizing initial. The test is one line: if a vowel follows the ng, it's an initial (this section); if nothing follows and it closes on itself, it's a rime (rimes chapter).

1 · The six basic vowels: ah ㄚ　aw ㄛ　uh ㄜ　eh ㄝ　ee ㄧ　u ㄨ

ah ㄚ

Start by seeing what that h is actually blocking. Suppose we spelled 肉 "meat" honestly as ba. Immediately there's a problem: in English, a word ending in a lone vowel — an open syllable — gets read with a long vowel. So ba comes out "Bay", as in the first syllable of baby or bacon, and the big open Hokkien "ah" has collapsed into an "ay".

A lone a has a second landmine, and it goes off when a consonant follows: bad is /bæd/, so by the same reflex tat becomes /tæt/ and sat becomes /sæt/ — that flat, short æ, which is nothing like the round, open Hokkien vowel. This one is especially easy to step on because there are real Hokkien words waiting right there: 塞 "block up" and 殺 "kill". Spell them lazily as tat and sat and the shapes look familiar while the vowel is simply wrong. So they are written taht. and saht. — the h is what locks the a down.

English had the fix ready long before we needed it: put a silent h behind the vowel. An American who sees bah lands instantly on the sheep's "Bah!" — or on Bahamas — and that full, open, short "ah" is nailed in place. Both landmines are cleared at once: no sliding off to "Bay", no flattening into the æ of tat and sat. Same story with 殺: saht. holds the vowel, whereas the lazy sat reads, to your eye, as the past tense of sit. You'd be telling someone to have a seat instead of saying "kill".

Adding a letter to lock a reading down — ba➔bah, sat➔saht — is what this entire chapter does, and it comes down to one sentence: adapt the spelling to the environment (open syllable, closed syllable, what follows), block the English brain's default reading path, and accept that the same sound may wear different coats in different places rather than let you say it wrong.

There's an important division of labour hiding here, and it's often misunderstood. This h has nothing to do with the -h of Tâi-lô, which marks a closed syllable. Here the h is a vowel fuse built for an English reader. It has no opinion about tone and no opinion about length. Its one job is to lock the vowel's quality to "ah" so it can't wander off.

Watch 肉 go from bare vowel to final spelling in three steps, and the division of labour plays itself out in front of you:

SpellingHow an American reads itProblem / result
ba"Bay"❌ open syllable — the vowel slides to "ay"
bah"Bah" (full open ah, one whole beat)✅ the vowel fuse is in — quality locked
bah."Bah!" (same ah, cut to half a beat)🔥 add the period — length halved = Clip tone

The step from row two to row three is the one to watch. Going from bah to bah., the vowel does not change by a hair — still that same ah. All that changed is a period on the end: quality untouched, duration halved. That is the cleanest demonstration of the principle that letters carry quality and marks carry length. The h guards the "ah" the whole way through; the period cuts one beat into half a beat. Separate those two ideas and the whole system opens up.

So remember: the h in ah is there for the vowel's quality, not for shortness of duration. Quality and length are two different dimensions — one belongs to the letters, the other to the marks. Every rime below is an extension of that one rule.

aw ㄛ

A lone o carries two layers of trouble. In an open syllable (o at the end, like bo, ho) you produce the vowel of go, no, so — which, and this is worth being precise about, is not a single vowel at all. It's a diphthong: your mouth starts at the half-open "aw" position and glides forward, rounding, toward "oo". Two movements glued together. Hokkien has no such glide. Write the letter o and an English-reading brain grows that "oo" tail automatically, producing a sound the language doesn't contain — which is the root cause of most confusion about this vowel.

With a consonant after it, it's worse. American English has a very strong habit that linguists call the father-bother merger: a short o before a consonant is read as an open "ah". Hot, top and rock all have it. So if 毒 "poison" were spelled ttok, you'd read it on the model of rock and lock — and the vowel would come out as "ah" instead.

Changing letters doesn't save you either. ook (spelling it ttook) is welded in your head to book and look, the rounded /ʊ/; you'd escape the "ah" only to land on "oo". ou (ttouk) is worse still, because ou in English is wildly unstable — soul, soup, tough, thought are four different vowels — so you'd most likely guess "ow". And Tâi-lô's oo? Just as unguessable: the oo of book is short /ʊ/, the oo of soon is long /u/, and neither face is the Hokkien vowel.

The only stable choice is aw. Think law, saw, hawk: aw sits solidly at /ɔ/ in your head, and — crucially — it doesn't care whether a consonant follows. The "o becomes ah" habit only attacks the letter o; it can't touch aw. So hawk stays "hawk" and never becomes "hahk". Where o, oo, ok, ook and ou are all minefields, aw is solid ground: see aw, say "aw". Examples: 黑 "black" aw¯ (a zero initial — the rime is the whole word), 路 "road" law+ (identical in shape to its own anchor, law), 苦 "bitter" kaw\, 數 "number" saw_ (again identical to its anchor, saw).

uh ㄜ (the central vowel)

A lone e is always read as a long "ee" by English instinct — he, me, she. Force this vowel onto a bare e and you'll simply produce "ee", never the central vowel Hokkien wants. (Tâi-lô writes this sound as a bare o, which fares no better with your instincts: in go and no the o glides off into that long "oh" rather than staying put.)

So we need a combination that pins it down, and English hands you two perfect ones: the "Huh?" you say when the penny drops, and the "Duh!" you say when someone states the obvious. That uh is the Hokkien vowel. Examples: 課 "lesson" kuh_, 薄 "thin" ppuh+, 刀 "knife" ttuh¯.

eh ㄝ (the short front vowel)

A lone e in a closed syllable — bed, egg, wedge — is already correct: a short, front "eh". The trouble is that most Hokkien words end on the vowel itself, with no consonant to close them, and the moment the syllable is open your e slides off into a long "ee" (he, me, she). So we add a silent h to close the syllable artificially, reproducing the environment of bed and egg: written eh, read as a short "eh". Examples: 帝 "emperor" tteh_, 多 "many" tseh+, 雞 "chicken" kkeh¯.

A historical witness: why the word tea is Hokkien

Nothing shows better why a spelling has to fit the brain of its own era than the word you drink every day. In the seventeenth century, English merchants took tea out of Amoy and the Hokkien-speaking coast, and what their ears received was the local word: tteh~.

They wrote it down as tea — and at the time that was an excellent transcription, because in seventeenth-century English ea was not read as today's "ee" but as "ay": sea rhymed with "say", meat sounded like "mayt". Read tea cold, with the reflexes of 1650, and you get "Tay" — an accurate rendering of the Hokkien word.

Then, in the closing stages of the Great Vowel Shift, most ea words slid from "ay" to "ee", and tea decayed into today's "Tee". A few fossils survived the change without mutating: great and steak still say "ay".

The lesson: letters on a page are dead; the phonics instinct in a living brain is not. A spelling that was precise in 1650 became a visual lie two centuries later. Which is exactly why we now rebuild the word for a modern American as tteh~ — only today's eh can lock down what today's reader will say.

ee ㄧ

A lone i at the end of a word gets read as "I" — hi, pi — which is a completely different vowel. So it's doubled: ee, exactly as in see and bee. Read it as written. Examples: 米 "uncooked rice" bee\ (voiced initial), 比 "compare" ppee\ (voiceless initial) — a tidy minimal pair for the voiced/voiceless contrast you met in the last chapter.

One spelling, see, becomes a different word in each tone. Six of the seven can be filled with real words: 四 "four" see_ (Low), 是 "to be" see+ (Mid), 絲 "silk" see¯ (Tone 1), 時 "time" see~ (Tone 3), 始 "begin" see\ (Tone 4), 熾 "blazing" see. (Clip). The Rise slot, see/, isn't an oversight: no Hokkien word has Rise as its citation tone. Rise appears only as the product of a special sandhi, so that slot is empty by design, waiting for sandhi to sit in it. (If you want to hear what see/ tastes like, say 絲 three times over — see/ see+ see¯ — an emphatic form describing something finely, thread-like stranded. The first 絲, the one that climbs, is the Rise that shows up only in triples.)

u ㄨ — no trap

The "oo" in the middle of tofu and guru. Read it as written; no fuse needed here. Examples: 舞 "dance" bu\, 無 "not have" bu~ (same letters, different tone — a memorable pair), 有 "to have" u+ (zero initial, Mid; a word you'll use constantly).

2 · The remaining basic rimes: ai ㄞ　ao ㄠ　ahn ㄢ　ahng ㄤ　ng ㄥ

The bopomofo chart has thirteen rime symbols. Four of them (ㄚㄛㄜㄝ) were the section above. Three more — the "ay" diphthong, the "oh" diphthong, and the r-coloured one — don't exist in Hokkien at all; they're Mandarin's, and you can skip them outright. That leaves the ones below, after which we get to the compound rimes built on i- and u-.

One thing worth saying first: ㄞㄠㄢㄤ are themselves compounds — ah+i, ah+u, ah+n, ah+ng. They're built from blocks you already have, not new material. They're listed separately here only because the traditional chart counts each as its own symbol, and following that order makes them easier to look up.

2.1 · ai ㄞ and ao ㄠ

  • ai ㄞ: borrowed from place names you already say correctly — Taiwan, Taipei, Thai, banzai. A final ai reads as a clean "ai" on sight. (Never ay: the ay of day, say, play gives you "ay" instead — spell 海 "sea" as hay and you've said a different word.) Examples: 台 ttai~, 海 hai\.
  • ao ㄠ: borrowed from Ciao and Macao — that closing glide toward "aw". You may know it even better from names: Lao Tzu, Hsiao. (Never au: English reads au as "aw", as in autumn.) Examples: 到 "arrive" kkao_, 老 "old" lao+, 包 "wrap" ppao¯.

2.2 · ahn ㄢ

A bare an has the same landmine the bare a had: in the commonest words — can, man, plan, scan — it reads as a flat /æn/, not the round Hokkien vowel. So the same h locks it: ahn. Think of swan, one of the few English words where an keeps the open vowel; add the locking h and ahn is the authentic Hokkien rime. (For the same reason, Milan and Sudan are fair anchors — but never model it on man or pan, which are far more common and flatly wrong.) Examples: 層 "storey" tsahn_, 萬 "ten thousand" bahn+, 牽 "lead by the hand" kahn¯, 寒 "cold" hahn~.

2.3 · ahng ㄤ

This is the nasal partner of the last one: swap the final n for ng. Full open "ah" plus the back-of-the-mouth ng. Think Kong and gong, where the o is read with that open "ah" quality; keep the ng tail and you have it.

But don't over-extend the anchor. In bang and hang the a is the flat æ; in song, long and wrong the o is rounded toward "aw". Neither is the open Hokkien vowel — the openness you can find in Kong and gong is not reliably there in song and long, so don't blend them. With the h fuse you already know, written ahng, you no longer have to gamble on which English word happens to be accurate. Examples: 藏 "hide" chahng_, 望 "hope" bahng+, 工 "work" kkahng¯.

The whole difference between ahn and ahng is where the nasal lands — one at the tongue tip, one at the back of the mouth. Compare 蘭 "orchid" lahn~ with 人 "person" lahng~: identical initial, identical tone, and the only difference is -n versus -ng. Close at the tip and it's "orchid"; close at the back and it's "person".

2.4 · ng ㄥ: treat the ng as the vowel

Words like kkng_, ppng+ and sng¯ look wrong to an English reader for an obvious reason: where's the vowel? The answer is refreshingly direct — read the ng as the vowel of the word. This is genuinely a new move, not something your instincts supply, and nothing else has to be propped up in the vowel slot. In the bopomofo chart ㄥ has always been a full rime symbol, sitting in the same row as ㄚㄛㄜㄝ. Here it simply takes the vowel's chair itself: your nasal cavity vibrates, and that vibration is the entire rime.

Examples, one at each of the three basic heights: 囥 "store away" kkng_ (Low), 飯 "cooked rice" ppng+ (Mid), 酸 "sour" sng¯ (Tone 1). You don't even need an initial: 黃 "yellow" ng~ is the rime alone, and so is 秧 "rice seedling" ng¯. Treat the ng as the vowel and the presence or absence of an initial changes nothing.

"How do you pronounce a syllable with no vowel" sounds mystical, and for a monolingual English speaker this really is a new physical action — worth hearing a few times rather than reading. There's a reliable trick, though: hum it. Close your mouth and hum a line of a tune — that "mmm" is pure nasal. Once it's steady, part your lips slightly, relax your teeth, and let the sound come out through your nose. That's the ng rime.

3 · Compound rimes: the i- and u- families

Everything left is two vowels (or a vowel plus a nasal) glided together, built out of the two glides you already have — i and u. One family at a time.

3.1 · Most i- rimes keep the i

  • ia ㄧㄚ: borrowed from piano and media — that smooth glide, readable on sight, no effort required. (No h needed here: the i in front holds the a in place, so it can't slide off to "ay".)
  • iu ㄧㄨ: worth flagging, because Mandarin doesn't have this one. Borrow the end of view or calcium — one continuous glide, closing tight. (Not ew, which collides with too many English words.) Examples: 油 "oil" yu~, 球 "ball" kkiu~, 收 "receive" siu¯, 羊 "sheep" ngiu~.
  • iao ㄧㄠ: borrowed from Ciao and Xiao. (Not iau — English reads au as "aw", as in autumn, and the whole thing tips over.)

3.2 · yuh ㄧㄜ: this one takes y, not i

The i- rimes above all behaved. This one doesn't. ㄧㄜ is the glide i plus the central vowel "uh", which by the rules so far should give iuh — and there the trap opens. Spell 惜 "cherish" as siuh and an English reader sees the iu in the middle, thinks of "Sue", takes the u as the main vowel, and the central vowel is gone. Put i and u side by side and the u will steal the lead.

The fix is to write the glide as y instead. y is a glide, not a vowel — the sound at the front of yes — so it can't take the lead. It just carries you into the uh behind it, and the u stays where it belongs. So this rime is always written yuh:

Why 尿 is spelled differently: after the voiced g (a single letter, remember — the voiced series), the rule would give gyuh+. But in English the combination gy is read as a "j" almost without exception (gym, gyro), which destroys the word. So there is exactly one exception: after voiced g, the glide reverts to i — because gi has give and gift holding the g hard, with no drift toward "j". Hence giuh+, never gyuh+. You may be staring at ttyuh and kkyuh thinking they look impossible; English has no words beginning tty- or kky-. Don't let that stop you. You already learned tt and kk in the initials chapter (the air-locked stops of star and skate), and all that's happening here is a light y glide added behind one of them. Think of the mouth this way: while you're making the air-locked tt or kk, the body of your tongue is already in position for the y of yes — release the air and the sound glides out on its own. And because English has no such combination, there's no English word for you to grab and mispronounce it with. Do it twice along with a recording — lock, then glide — and it's yours.

3.3 · i- rimes that close on a nasal: in / ing / im / iam / iang / iong / yen

Nothing new here either: the glide i stays put and one of the nasal endings from the initials chapter is bolted on. Where the word has no initial consonant, the same h fuse you learned for ahn and ahng applies.

  • in ㄧㄣ: borrowed from pin, bin, tin. Example: 民 "the people" bin~.
  • ing ㄧㄥ: i plus ng in its rime-final job (section 2.4). Time to repeat the iron rule of this system: whenever you see ng, push it all the way back into a full back-of-the-mouth nasal, and don't let your English -ing habit (goin', singin') shrink it to an "-in". 升 "rise" sing¯ is the classic case: s + i + ng, and that final ng must be complete. The mnemonic: see ng, hold it at the back of the throat, don't let it slip forward to the gum ridge. Drill it as a minimal pair — 新 "new" sin¯ versus 升 "rise" sing¯: sin closes at the tongue tip, sing closes where song closes. Keep the ng at the back and the two never merge. Examples: 停 "stop" ting~, 拱 "arch" kking+.
  • im ㄧㄇ: i plus the lip-closing m. Examples: 淹 "flood" im¯, 沈 "sink" ttim~, 㔤 "rock up and down" sim_.
  • iam ㄧㄚㄇ: with no initial consonant it needs locking — write yahm, because an unlocked yam would give you the English æ. Examples: 閹 "castrate" yahm¯, 鹹 "salty" kkiam~.
  • iang ㄧㄤ: same logic; zero initial is written yahng. Examples: 央 "entreat" yahng¯, 涼 "cool" liang~, 誰 "who" siang\.
  • iong ㄧㄛㄥ: the tail of long. Plenty of words: 忠 "loyal" ttiong¯, 中 "middle" ttiong¯ (a homophone of 忠), 強 "strong" kkiong~; zero initial is written yong, as in 勇 "brave" yong\.

Looked at together, there's one rule: keep the i, copy the nasal ending you already know. The only ones needing a second thought are the three with no initial consonant (yahm, yahng, yong), which collide with English words you know and take the h where needed. in, ing and im hold no traps and need no extra machinery.

And then there's one special case that gets confused with in, but is really a three-part combination (i + eh + n).

yen ㄧㄝㄣ

Don't write it ian — you'd see the name Ian and say "Ee-un", two syllables, and the tight single beat of the Hokkien word falls apart. The answer is to borrow the Japanese currency, yen: in your head that's one solid syllable, and you'll get it right the first time. (Taiwan's official dictionary still mostly writes this group as "ian", but what people actually say is this yen. Don't let the spelling talk you back into two loose pieces.) Examples: 煙火 "fireworks" yen hueh\, 現 "appear" hyen_, 倦 "weary" syen+, 天 "sky" tyen¯, 剪 "cut" tsyen\.

The usual error is stretching 煙 into a loose "ee-an". yen is one syllable in one breath. Don't split it.

3.4 · The u- family

  • oon ㄨㄣ: whatever you do, not un. Spell 滾 "boil" as kkun and you'll immediately apply sun, fun, run — the "oo" eaten alive by an "uh", and the word is wrecked. The answer is the doubled oon: moon, spoon, noon. See oon and your lips round up naturally into "oo"; add the final n and you land exactly on the target. Examples: 滾 kkoon\, 問 "ask" boon+, 孫 "grandchild" soon¯, 熏 "to smoke" hoon¯ (never hun — you'd read it like hunt or honey).
  • ua ㄨㄚ: think of the middle of guava or quack. Examples: 花 "flower" hua¯, 瓜 "melon" kkua¯. (Inside a word, don't write wa — a spelling like hwa makes you work the w too hard; hua flows better. At the very front of a word a lone wa is fine, as in 蛙 "frog".)
  • ueh ㄨㄝ (written ue when it takes a following syllable in sandhi): since ㄝ is eh, ㄨ+ㄝ is ueh. Borrow the short vowel of "wedge", which locks it firmly and stops it drifting toward "ui". Examples: 火 "fire" hueh\, 話 "speech" ueh+. (Not oi — some older spellings used it, but you'd read it as the vowel of boy.)
  • ui ㄨㄧ: Mandarin has no such syllable. Borrow the middle of suicide or fluid — lips rounded, then opening, which is exactly the movement. Examples: 水 "water" tsui\, 鬼 "ghost" kkui\, 梅 "plum" mui~.
  • uai ㄨㄞ: just ua + ai run together; borrow the feel of Kuwait and glide all the way through. Examples: 歪 "crooked" uai¯, 乖 "well-behaved" kkuai¯.
  • ㄨㄥ ➔ pronounced "awng", written ong: this one deserves a flag, especially if you've studied Mandarin. Mandarin speakers instinctively look for a "u" in this slot, but the Hokkien sound here is a pure "aw" + ng with no trace of "oo" in it. (The Mandarin spelling is an artefact: Mandarin's glides are only i, u and ü — there is no "aw" glide available — so the chart borrows the ready-made ㄨㄥ to write it. The spelling says one thing; the mouth does another.) Hokkien skips the detour and writes ong — the tail of song and long. Examples: 翁 "husband" ong¯ (the rime alone), 壯 "robust" tsong_, 搪 "push against" ttong+, 霜 "frost" song¯ (identical in shape to song, easy to remember).

4 · Closing rimes: -p, -t, -k, -m

Hokkien has a class of syllables whose way of ending is exotic from a Mandarin point of view and completely ordinary from yours. In English cat, cap and back, you never actually release that final consonant — the tongue or the lips move into position, the air stops, and that's the end of the word. Hokkien does the same, and this book calls the result a closing rime, because that is what it is: part of the rime.

Tradition calls it the "entering tone", a name a thousand years old that has spent those thousand years mistaking a way of closing for a kind of tone — the word "tone" in the label being the root of the error. We'll use the accurate name instead. And let's dismantle the usual misunderstanding right away: a closing rime is about how the syllable ends, not about how fast or short it is. 毒 "poison" ttawk+ holds a steady, level Mid; the 骨 of 骨頭 "bone" kkut\ holds a full Tone 4 — neither gets rushed or clipped just because the air is locked at the end. Length belongs to the tone, closing belongs to the rime. Four endings:

  • -p (lips snap shut): the way American top ends. 壓 "press" ahp., 答 "answer" ttahp.
  • -t (tongue tip to the ridge): the way cat ends. 揮 "wave" eht+, 一 "one" it.
  • -k (tongue back to the throat): the way American rock ends. 毒 "poison" ttawk+, 沃 "to water" ahk.
  • -m (lips closed, air through the nose): the way ham and hum end. As with ahn and ahng, an "ah" before -m takes the h — write ahm, never bare am: 含 "contain" kkahm~, 淡 "bland" ttahm+. With i as the main vowel no fuse is needed: 飲 "drink" lim¯. One difference from the other three: with -m the air isn't locked off, it keeps flowing out through the nose. That distinction earns its keep in the tones chapter.

Mandarin has none of these four endings. For you they're second nature — ham, cat, cap, rock are words you say every day, and your mouth has been making the movement all along without anyone naming it.

The spelling is plain: put p, t or k straight after the rime. Each vowel keeps its own locking device — the "ah" family locks with h (ahp, ahk, aht), the "aw" family locks with aw (awp, awk, awt), and ong is stable on its own and needs no lock. See it, say it, close and stop.

Closing rimes take tones like anything else. Start with four: two words, four compounds, and you have them.

  • 排骨 "pork ribs" kkut. — Clip: mid, half a beat, tapped and released
  • 骨頭 "bone" kkut\ — Tone 4: So→Mi, sliding down from the top
  • 很滑 "very slippery" kkut+ — Mid: one full beat, held level
  • 滑溜 "slick" kkut_ — Low: flat and low, walking along the floor

Same 骨, same 滑 — put them in a different compound and the tone changes, while the closing stays exactly as it was. Take away one thing here: a closing rime is a way of ending the rime, not a tone. The period is the Clip tone (which governs length); every tone in the language can sit on a closing rime, and the p/t/k (which governs closing) is a separate matter entirely. What's more, those four heights come and go strictly by the general sandhi rules in the tones chapter — closing-rime words introduce no rules of their own and no clauses to memorize. (And four is not the limit; the full account is in the tones chapter.)

One more word makes the whole division of labour visible at once: 搏 "wrestle" bawk. — three things happening in a single syllable, a voiced initial (b), a closing rime (-k), and the Clip tone (the period). None of the three interferes with the others, which proves that voicing, closing and tone are three independent dimensions, freely combinable. In particular, it shows you that a Clip-tone word can perfectly well be voiced — one more demonstration that a closing rime is just how the rime ends.

Tones, in full

Reading Hokkien tones off a staff the whole world already uses

The earlier chapter only taught you to recognize the marks. This one gets the seven tones actually sung, and deals with what happens to them when words are strung together. A tone is nothing but pitch — and pitch is best written on a staff.

One example to show how much weight tones carry in this language. The single syllable ttee, differing in nothing but tone, is three completely unrelated words: 戴 "wear" ttee_ (Low), 箸 "chopsticks" ttee+ (Mid), 豬 "pig" ttee¯ (Tone 1). Identical letters, unrelated meanings, and the only thing standing between them is the little mark on the end.

0 · One set of tones, three notations

Before we start, a word about notation. These seven tones have three interchangeable ways of being written in this system. First, the marks used throughout this book — in the order Low, Mid, 1, 2, 3, 4, Clip: _ + ¯ / ~ \ . Second, a bopomofo-style set (_, +, ¯, ˊ, ˇ, ˋ, ˙) for readers cross-referencing Taiwanese teaching materials. Third, an all-digit set, which is the fastest thing to type. All three describe the same seven tones and convert freely.

Use the marks for learning and teaching — their shape is the pitch. Use the digits for typing, since each mark has a number you can hit directly without hunting for a special character: Low _ = 9, Mid + = 0, Tone 1 ¯ = 1, Tone 2 / = 2, Tone 3 ~ = 3, Tone 4 \ = 4, Clip . = 5. So see¯ = see1, ah_ = ah9, kkut. = kkut5.

Two things not to confuse with Tâi-lô. First, the digit 5 here is simply the typing code for the Clip tone (just as 9 is Low and 0 is Mid); it has nothing to do with Tâi-lô's "tone 5". Second, you will often hear the phrase "seven tones in eight slots". That "seven" is not the same seven as ours by anything other than coincidence: that system's "eight" comes from counting closing rimes as separate tone classes, whereas our seven marks are pure pitch. Same number, different meaning — don't map one onto the other.

1 · Start with three tones: Do, Mi, So

What is a tone? Even as an English speaker you already use two Hokkien tones daily. Take see: "See?" as a question flicks up; "I see." as a statement presses down. That flick and that press are tones — an instruction laid on top of a sound. The rising question is see/ (Tone 2, Rise); the falling statement is see\ (Tone 4, Fall). In English the instruction only changes your attitude. In Hokkien it can change the entire word.

Start with three. Each has a tone mark written after the spelling, telling you what height to hit, and when you pronounce the word you are obeying that instruction: Low is _, Mid is +, High is ¯.

Low, do — the mark _: see_ carries an order that says "low", and on that order see sinks to the bottom, to do. That see_ is the word 四 "four".

Mid, mi — the mark +: see+ carries an order that says "mid", and see lands dead centre, on mi. That see+ is 是 "to be".

High, so — the mark ¯: see¯ carries an order that says "high", and see goes up to so and holds there without sagging. That see¯ is 絲 "silk".

So when you meet a spelling, don't rush into sound — read the mark first, then speak: see_, see+, see¯ (do, mi, so). (You can also memorize the three heights through three ready-made words that happen to sit on them: kkeh_, ttiong+, kkuh¯.)

Now run the same three on a single vowel against the staff below: ah_, ah+, ah¯ — do, mi, so.

Now the three heights have an anchor that could hardly be clearer: the staff the whole world shares. Do, mi, so — anyone can hum them, and nobody has to guess. Once more: do, mi, so — ah_, ah+, ah¯.

You may be thinking: "I'm not a singer — how am I supposed to hit the same pitch every time?" You don't have to. Try the body method: each of the three heights has a switch, and if you flip the right one the pitch arrives by itself.

do (low): resonate in the chest. Sink the voice into your chest, below your throat. (It helps to drop your eyes.) Examples: see_, ah_.

mi (mid): resonate dead centre. Flat face, push it straight forward. (It helps to look straight ahead.) Examples: see+, ah+.

so (high): resonate at the crown. Send the voice out through the top of your head. (It helps to look up.) Examples: see¯, ah¯.

The higher the pitch, the higher in the body it is produced — your own body is a pitch ruler. Flip the right switch and the height arrives on its own; you don't have to be a singer. Try it: low, mid, high — ah_, ah+, ah¯ — do, mi, so.

2 · The seven tones, sung one at a time

Low, mid and high are standing steady on the body (chest, centre, crown). Now we complete the set — and the most important sentence in the chapter comes first: every tone mark is an order, telling you what height to hit and how: hold it level, slide it, or tap it and stop. See the mark, obey it, then speak.

If you've studied Mandarin you can file the seven as Low, Mid, 1, 2, 3, 4, Clip — Mandarin's own four tones plus its light tone, with Low and Mid added on top. That's the entire difference: Hokkien has exactly those two extra tones, no more.

If Mandarin tone numbers mean nothing to you, use the names instead: Low, Mid, High, Rise, Dip, Fall, Clip — each name says what the tone does (high is high, rise goes up, dip sinks and lifts, fall goes down, clip taps once and stops), so it sticks by itself.

Read the chart alongside the marks: every tone sits at its own place on the staff, and the marks are _, +, ¯, /, ~, \, . — all easy to type on an ordinary keyboard, with no input method installed. (The Tone 1 ¯ isn't on your keyboard: when typing, use the digit code from section 0, or simply leave it off — anyone who reads this system knows an unmarked syllable is Tone 1.) Run the seven on "ah" while you look at the chart: ah_ ah+ ah¯ ah/ ah~ ah\ ah.

Low, Mid and Tone 1 were covered above. Here are the other four, one at a time.

Rise (Tone 2) | from mid, climbing | /
Up from the middle — the "Huh?" you say when you didn't catch something: starts mid, flicks up at the end. That contour is a rise by nature. It is rarely a citation tone in Hokkien and shows up mainly under emphasis (you'll meet it in a moment with the tripled 絲). The mark / is the direction of travel.

Dip (Tone 3) | sink first, then hook up | ~
The most flavourful of the seven: down to the bottom, then hooked back up from there. Think of the interested "Uh-huh?" you give someone telling you gossip, with the tail lifting. The wave in the mark ~ is that down-then-up shape.

Fall (Tone 4) | pitch sliding down from the top (5→3) | \
Anchor it on something you do without thinking: a flat, final "No." — starts high, drops fast, no coming back. "Stop." has the same shape. Borrow its pitch contour, not its mouth — the vowel of no is a glide, whereas each Hokkien word keeps its own rime. What slides is the pitch. It is not extra force, and it needn't be short. The mark \ is the path it takes.

Clip (light tone) | half a beat and done | .
An unstressed, quickly passed-over note, like a piano staccato: touch it and let go. The mark is a single dot.

And now the one drill that matters — the Hokkien tone scale. Take one syllable and sing all seven in fixed order:

ah_ ah+ ah¯ ah/ ah~ ah\ ah. (Low, Mid, High, Rise, Dip, Fall, Clip)

Sing it daily, the way a musician runs scales, until you don't have to think. When it's steady, run it again with an initial attached (kkah_ kkah+ kkah¯ kkah/ kkah~ kkah\ kkah.). Once that string is fluent your ear owns seven calibration points — so that when you hear a Hokkien speaker say 紅紅紅 "unbelievably red", ahng/ ahng+ ahng~, you can hear three identical words sung at three different heights: the first is that Rise which surfaces only under emphasis; the second sits at Mid; the third holds its citation Dip, sinking and lifting. The moment you can hear those three slots, the tones are genuinely yours.

Closing rimes sit in the same seven-tone system — the tail stays locked on -p/-t/-k while the pitch does whatever it likes. Sing all seven shapes: kkut_ kkut+ kkut¯ kkut/ kkut~ kkut\ kkut. (which of those have ready-made words, and which exists only as an exercise, is section 6).

Singing an empty "ah" only takes you so far. Run it once more with real words — the same see, tone by tone: 四 "four" see_, 是 "to be" see+, 絲 "silk" see¯… every tone has a word to fill it. 3 · A crucial clarification: Tone 3 is not the Mandarin second tone

This is the single easiest thing for a Mandarin speaker to get wrong, and the one most worth hearing clearly at the start.

Mandarin's second tone (as in lái "come") rises from the middle; it starts at mid pitch. Hokkien's Tone 3 does something else: it starts at the very bottom, sinks, and then lifts, beginning down at do. The two sound very different — one is raising your arm, the other is crouching before a jump. Many Hokkien words carry this sink-then-lift shape as their citation tone: 紅 "red", 寒 "cold", 茶 "tea". (A rough but useful rule of thumb to start from: words that take the second tone in Mandarin are usually Tone 3 in Hokkien. Not universally, but it's a good enough opening bet.)

The straightforward mid-to-high rise — which is what the Mandarin second tone does — is comparatively rare in Hokkien, surfacing only under emphasis, as in the tripled 紅紅紅 "unbelievably red". And if anyone insists that the citation tone of 紅 is a Rise, ask them this: in 紅紅紅 all three syllables are pronounced differently — so what tone is the first one?

4 · Sandhi: tones change in connected speech, and one line covers it

Brace yourself for one fact about Hokkien: a word said alone has one tone, but strung together with other words it usually takes a different one. That's tone sandhi. As for which words change — a sentence breaks into chunks of meaning, and the last syllable of each chunk keeps its citation tone while the rest shift, so the unshifted syllables in a sentence are usually more than just the final one. How the chunks get drawn is a topic of its own.

Don't panic: sandhi is natural, not a rule to be memorized. It's like saying two English words quickly and letting them run together — your mouth reaches for the next word and the tone turns on its own. Other systems make this painful by asking you to memorize a long chain of numbers. Here you memorize one line: "Lie low, make one point." Get the feeling behind that sentence and there's nothing to cram.

Lie low — the tone sinks a step

Hokkien has two tones Mandarin doesn't: the low do and the mid mi. Put them side by side and you can hear it — Mandarin speech runs relatively high, Hokkien relatively low, with a built-in tendency to keep its head down. That tendency shows up in sandhi: the voice naturally sinks one step. High drops to mid, mid drops to low.

  • Tone 1 (High) → Mid
  • Mid → Low
  • Low (already at the floor) → nowhere left to sink, so it rebounds into Tone 4, falling from the top
  • Clip (the lightest) → becomes the heaviest, Tone 4

In one line: whatever was high sinks a step; whatever hits the floor bounces back up to Tone 4. That's "lie low" — a low-profile personality, tones drifting downward.

One point — a gliding tone with two targets is reduced to one

Tone 4 and Tone 3 are the gliding tones. A gliding tone has two pitch targets, a start and an end (on the 1-to-5 pitch scale, 5 highest): Tone 4 is 53 (sliding from 5 down to 3 — two targets); Tone 3 is 113 (sitting at 1, then lifting to 3 — two targets). What happens in sandhi is reduction: to get to the next word promptly, the two targets collapse into one.

  • Tone 4 (53, two targets) → keep one, the 5 → level and high, i.e. Tone 1
  • Tone 3 (113, two targets) → keep one, the 3 → level at mi, i.e. Mid

Note what's being reduced: the number of targets, not the effort. The glide collapses to a single target; the sound isn't slacking off, it's simply settling at one height. That's "one point" — after sandhi, one syllable has one pitch target.

And Tone 2? Tone 2 isn't in this system. It isn't produced by ordinary sandhi at all — it only surfaces under emphasis (the first syllable of an emphatic triple, or the coaxing tone you use with someone close to you). Normal connected speech never generates it. While you're drilling sandhi, ignore it.

That one line — "Lie low, make one point" — is the whole of Hokkien tone sandhi. (If you ever hear that another system requires memorizing a code like 5→7→3→2→1→7, that's their approach, not one you need here.)

5 · The Clip tone must always be marked

Here is something other systems routinely fail to do and this one insists on: the Clip tone is always written out, with that small dot .

Why the insistence? Because Clip is a real tone in Hokkien — a great many words simply have it (half a beat at mi: length halved, pitch mid). Leave it unmarked and there is no way to see that it's Clip, so it gets read on some other tone and the meaning goes with it. Native speakers know by instinct which words are clipped; a learner needs it written down. So in this system, when you see ., halve the length, touch the note and release — what's cut is the beat, not the volume.

A word for anyone used to the older way of talking about this. Teachers often say "say this one lightly, that one heavily" — but a tone only ever has two dimensions: pitch and length. The "light" of the Clip tone is halved length; the "heavy" of Tone 4 is a falling pitch. Force has never been a dimension of tone. Example: someone can speak very sharply, 殺 saht. — a Clip-tone word that is anything but quiet; it's loud and forceful. So much for "light" meaning soft.

To hear Tone 4 and Clip side by side, say 肉肉肉 in a row — bah\ bah\ bah.: the first two are Tone 4, pitch falling from the top; the last is Clip, stopping at half a beat. Back to back, the difference is unmistakable. These two can be hard to tell apart in practice; comparing them against the seven-tone chart is one way, and the body method is another —

Tone 4: start at the highest point, the crown, and drop the instant the sound begins. Catch that "starting at the top of the head and falling" feeling and Tone 4 comes out right.
Clip: stay right at the mid position (looking straight ahead, exactly as for Mid) and release as soon as you sound it. One syllable, ttawk, makes the three obvious at once: 啄 "peck" ttawk. (Clip), 毒 "poison" ttawk+ (Mid), and, in coastal accents, 毒 ttawk\ (Tone 4).

6 · How many tones can a closing rime carry? Seven — all of them

The rimes chapter established that a closing rime is a way of ending — the air locked at -p, -t or -k. It is not a tone, and it is not necessarily short. From that follows something very simple that the old word "entering tone" has obscured for a thousand years: a closing-rime word, like any other word, has all seven tones available to it. Not one is missing. Claims like "the entering tone has only two tones" are pseudo-questions that arise only after you have mistaken a way of closing for a kind of tone.

First, head off a likely confusion: the h in this system is a vowel fuse. It locks vowel quality and touches neither the tone nor the ending. It merely looks like Tâi-lô's -h, which is a tone marker. Same letter, different dimension — don't map one onto the other.

Note too that "closing rime" is not the same set of words as the official "entering tone". The Ministry classes words like 食 "eat" as entering-tone; in this system they are ordinary open syllables and take all seven tones like anything else — 食 is simply Mid, tsia+ (Tone 4, tsia\, in coastal accents). What this section means by a closing rime is only the four genuine closing endings: -p, -t, -k and -m. And a Clip tone is simply a Clip tone.

In the end a closing rime is part of the rime — the air stopped on -p/-t/-k/-m. It establishes no special "tone" of its own and has no sandhi rules of its own. Closing-rime words live in the same seven-tone system as everything else, and the closing does not interfere with the tone.

One syllable, kkut, sings six different tones in ordinary everyday compounds:

The one tone absent from kkut is Tone 3, which has to sink and then turn. This is not because a stop ending chokes it off; no rule forbids it. In practice, the hard "stop" of -p/-t/-k doesn't sit comfortably with Tone 3's turn, so no word happens to have that combination. Six heights on kkut is simply what the language happens to have filled in — not a prohibition. (-m is different: it closes on a nasal, which is soft enough to turn, and the words are really there — 南 "south" lahm~ fills all seven.)

A closing-rime word keeps its ending locked and can in principle be sung at all seven heights, though not every word supplies an example of each. Sing whatever the mark tells you, and don't memorize rules of the form "the entering tone may only take tones X and Y" — because they aren't true. One example to close on: the sentence 一生只愛你一人 "all my life I've loved only you" contains two instances of 一, and they are two different sounds — the 一 of 一生 is the literary reading it., and the 一 of 一人 is the colloquial reading tsit+ (each shifting in context: the first reads Tone 4, the second Low). Mark them clearly and the line reads exactly right.

7 · The best practice of all: Hokkien songs

Finally, the most natural drill there is: listen to Hokkien songs and sing along.

Tones are pitch, and singing spells pitch out unmistakably — stretched long enough that you have time to feel it. Singing along with a Hokkien song is repeated practice at those contours, and especially at that sink-then-lift Tone 3, which a melody holds long enough for you to catch its rise out of the bottom.

For the low, brooding flavour, try 〈雪中紅〉 "Red in the Snow" — a song built largely on low, sinking Tone 3 endings, and it sings the distance and the sorrow into you.

Compared with staring at a table of pitch values, letting a melody teach your ear and throat is far easier and sticks far better.

One last easter egg: hold the Low tone and don't let go — see___ — and you're singing the chanted patter style of Taiwanese variety storytelling. Low do is one of the most familiar channels in a Taiwanese ear. (Repeating the mark to show length is a transcription device for performance only; ordinary writing marks the tone class, not duration.)

End of the tones. That completes the three core blocks of this system — initials, rimes and tones. Next, putting it all together and reading actual sentences.

Putting it together

Initials, rimes and tones assembled into ten real sentences

You can already read the initials, the rimes and the tone marks. This chapter teaches nothing new — it just puts them together so you can look at the letters and say a real Hokkien sentence. One principle: say what you see.

One convention to add, the hyphen: a syllable with - after it shifts once in this sentence — slide it one step per "Lie low, make one point". A syllable without a hyphen is read on the tone marked. The first line of each example gives the citation tones with the hyphens; the "spoken" line spells out what actually comes out of your mouth after the shifts. Read the spoken line aloud and you're right. Which syllable shifts, and where the sentence breaks, is all marked for you.

1. 你好 "Hello"　lee\-huh\｜spoken: lee¯ huh\

  • 你 lee\ — l (look) + ee (the vowel of see); it carries the hyphen, so Tone 4 becomes Tone 1 and parks up high
  • 好 huh\ — h (how) + uh (the vowel of "Huh?") + \, its citation tone, dropping

One level, one drop: 你 hangs flat up top, 好 slides down from the top. That's "hello".

2. 多謝 "Thank you"　ttuh¯-sia+｜spoken: ttuh+ sia+

  • 多 ttuh¯ — air-locked tt + uh; Tone 1 shifts to Mid
  • 謝 sia+ — the s palatalizes automatically before ia; citation Mid

Both syllables land flat on mi — a polite, level close.

3. 食飽未？ "Have you eaten?" — the most common greeting in Taiwan　tsia+-ppah\ bueh+-｜spoken: tsia_ ppah\ bueh_

  • 食 tsia+ — ts before ia glides into the palatal automatically; Mid shifts to Low, so you read it down at do
  • 飽 ppah\ — air-locked pp + ah; no hyphen, so citation tone — 食飽 "eaten full" is one unit of meaning and 飽 is its last syllable. The Tone 4 takes the pitch from 5 down to 3; what falls is the pitch, not stress (the question is about the state of being full)
  • 未 bueh+- — voiced b + ueh; a special sentence-final shift, read as a plain Low, flat at do — call it a tone of voice

Three different treatments in one short sentence: 食 shifts, 飽 holds its citation tone, 未 shifts to close. That is the breathing rhythm of spoken Hokkien.

4. 好食 "Delicious"　huh\-tsia+｜spoken: huh¯ tsia+

  • 好 huh\ — Tone 4 shifts to Tone 1, hoisted high
  • 食 tsia+ — citation Mid (note: in coastal accents, and in the Hokkien of Southeast Asia, this word is usually Tone 4, tsia\)

Compare with sentence 3: the same 食, shifting at the front of a phrase and holding its citation tone at the end. Position decides everything.

5. 飲茶 "Drink tea"　lim¯-tteh~｜spoken: lim+ tteh~

  • 飲 lim¯ — l + im (the -m closes honestly on shut lips); Tone 1 shifts to Mid
  • 茶 tteh~ — air-locked tt + eh + ~: sink to do, then hook up — the tastiest demonstration of Tone 3 there is

6. 很美 "Really beautiful"　tsin-sui\｜spoken: tsin+ sui\

  • 很 tsin — ts + in (as in pin); Tone 1 (unmarked) shifts to Mid
  • 美 sui\ — s + ui (the middle of fluid); citation Tone 4, dropping. (美 has two readings: colloquial sui\, literary bee\)

7. 我知影 "I know"　gua\-tsai-ngia\｜spoken: gua¯ tsai+ ngia\

  • 我 gua\ — voiced g (as in go), folds buzzing before the mouth opens; Tone 4 shifts to Tone 1
  • 知 tsai — ts + ai (Taiwan); Tone 1 shifts to Mid
  • 影 ngia\ — ng standing at the front as an initial (the "song-ah" trick); citation Tone 4 closes it

8. 我是台灣人 "I am Taiwanese"　gua\-see+-ttai~-uan¯-lahng~｜spoken: gua¯ see_ ttai+ uan+ lahng~

  • 我 gua\ — Tone 4 shifts to Tone 1
  • 是 see+ — s before ee rides up to the palatal; Mid shifts to Low, flat at do
  • 台 ttai~ — Tone 3 shifts and settles on Mid
  • 灣 uan¯ — zero initial, citation Tone 1. By the rule, a shifting Tone 1 goes to Mid. Curiously, though the citation tone is Tone 1, most people say 台灣 with 灣 on Tone 3 (uan~) — a piece of historical memory, perhaps
  • 人 lahng~ — l + ahng; citation Tone 3, sinking then lifting, holding the whole sentence steady at the close

9. 一生只愛你一人 "All my life I have loved only you"　it.-sing¯ tsee\-ai_ lee\-tsit+-lahng~｜spoken: it\ sing¯ tsee¯ ai_ lee¯ tsit_ lahng~

  • What to watch here: the two instances of 一 are the literary and colloquial readings, and each shifts differently. One line demonstrates the whole case for marking things clearly.
  • 一 (in 一生) it. — literary reading, citation Clip; as the first syllable of 一生 "a lifetime" it shifts to Tone 4.
  • 生 sing¯ — Tone 1, and as the last syllable of 一生 it holds its citation tone.
  • 只 tsee\, 愛 ai_, 你 lee\ — the middle runs on each word's own citation tone and shift.
  • 一 (in 一人) tsit+ — colloquial reading, citation Mid; as the first syllable of 一人 "one person" it shifts to Low.
  • 人 lahng~ — citation Tone 3, sinking then lifting, holding the close.

10. 靠山山會崩 "Lean on a mountain and the mountain will collapse" — one sentence, several sandhi groups　kuh_-sngua¯ sngua¯ eh+-ppahng¯｜spoken: kuh\ sngua¯ sngua¯ eh_ ppahng¯

  • The sentence breaks into three chunks: 靠山 "lean on a mountain" | 山 "the mountain" | 會崩 "will collapse". The last syllable of each chunk holds its citation tone — 山, 山 and 崩 are all Tone 1; the rest shift — 靠 (Low→4) and 會 (Mid→Low).
  • The middle 山 is the subject and forms a chunk by itself, so it doesn't shift. Which is why more than just the final syllable keeps its citation tone: as the tones chapter said, a sentence splits into several sandhi groups, and each group has its own unshifted syllable.

Closing

This isn't only a spelling. It's an engine.

1 · What you're holding was tailored for you

You can now read Hokkien with this system. One last thing worth telling you: on the surface this is "the English edition", but underneath it is an engine designed to be re-dressed.

The structure looks like this:

The real sounds of Hokkien (fixed) → the 135 tone-and-analysis engine (fixed) → letters and landmine warnings matched to your native instincts (swappable)

The two inner layers — the actual pronunciation, and the do-mi-so engine of tones and sandhi — never move. The only thing that changes is the outermost coat: whose ear this spelling is speaking to.

The coat you were handed is cut for the instincts of a native English speaker. The same Hokkien sound wears one coat for an American and a different one for a French speaker — because the landmines each of them has to avoid, and the native instincts each of them can borrow, are completely different.

2 · Change the native language, re-cut the coat

Take an example. Suppose a French speaker wants to learn Hokkien. Nothing about the system needs rewriting — same engine, same Hokkien sounds. Only the outermost layer, the anchors and the landmines, has to be redrawn, because French reading instincts differ sharply from English ones:

  • Aspirated versus unaspirated swap difficulty. French p, t and k are already unaspirated — the very set you had to learn through spy, star and skate comes free to a French speaker. What has to be taught to them instead is the aspirated set.
  • The h stops working. French h is silent (hôtel), so the ㄏ that this edition teaches through how is useless there — that consonant becomes their problem instead of yours.
  • Nasalization turns into a home game. The nasalized sounds some learners struggle with are second nature to a language full of nasal vowels (bon, vin, blanc). What is one of the harder bones in this edition may be the easiest section in the French one.

One engine; a French, Japanese or Spanish reader each gets a skin cut to fit:

Hokkien's real sounds (fixed) → the 135 tone engine (fixed) → a coat cut from the reader's native language (pluggable)

Every edition asks the same two questions — the landmines (where native instinct will misread the page) and the free lifts (what the native language already supplies):

🇺🇸 English (this book)
Landmines: ph read as /f/; e read as "ee"; o read as "oh"
Free lifts: voiced b and g come for free; trained phonics reflexes; an ear raised on do-mi-so

🇫🇷 French
Landmines: silent h (ㄏ needs another route); ch read as /ʃ/; u read as ü
Free lifts: nasal vowels are everyday (bon, vin, blanc) — Hokkien nasalization is a gift; unaspirated p, t, k are native

🇪🇸 Spanish
Landmines: silent h; j read as a throaty /x/; z read as /s/ or /θ/
Free lifts: five clean, stable vowels; voiced b, d, g for free; the diphthongs ai and au already exist

🇩🇪 German
Landmines: w read as /v/; ei read as "ai"; final voiced consonants devoice automatically (Tag said as "tak")
Free lifts: z is natively /ts/; ng is native (singen); final -p/-t/-k/-m closures are daily habit (Tag, Stopp, am) — the closing-rime gesture comes ready-made

🇯🇵 Japanese
Landmines: no -n / -ng distinction — 蘭 "orchid" (lahn~) comes out as 人 "person" (lahng~); Roman letters automatically trigger kana timing; l and r merge
Free lifts: the geminate っ is a ready-made instinct for the closing-rime gesture; the voiced/voiceless contrast (か/が) is native; the mora sense helps with length

Each coat has its own landmines and its own free lifts — which is why a coat cannot be translated, only re-cut. The frame already exists; each new edition needs one thing: a person with native-level instincts in that language to refill the anchors and the landmines. (The English edition in your hands was made exactly that way: someone guarding the authentic Hokkien sounds, working with an understanding of English phonics, polishing from both sides. The table above is illustrative; each edition's actual spellings get settled against that language's own reading reflexes.)

3 · Four spellings, four readerships

Seeing it as "whose ear was this coat cut for" also lets you understand at a glance who each existing Hokkien spelling was born to serve. None of them is wrong; they simply have different readers:

  • Pe̍h-ōe-jī (POJ): born for nineteenth-century missionaries.
  • Tâi-lô: born for linguists and academic standardization.
  • Hanyu Pinyin: born for Mandarin speakers annotating Chinese characters.
  • This system: born for you — someone with nothing but English reading instincts who wants to start speaking Hokkien.

The first three were built to serve someone who is not you today. That's not their fault; they were born into different needs. Yours was built for exactly one purpose: see it, say it right, and speak with confidence.

Please remember: behind that ease is someone who took the hard parts off your plate. Every sound in the language, every sandhi rule, every decision about why one letter was used and another refused, came out of years of grinding and was proofread one word at a time.
